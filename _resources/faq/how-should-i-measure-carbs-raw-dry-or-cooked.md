The most reliable method is to measure carbohydrate foods in the state that best matches how you planned them. For rice, oats, pasta, potatoes, beans, lentils, and similar foods, that usually means choosing one standard and using it consistently: raw, dry, or cooked.

The reason this matters is water. Dry rice, cooked rice, and overcooked rice can all represent very different weights for the same amount of actual food energy. Pasta changes weight depending on how long it is boiled. Potatoes can lose water when roasted or gain water when boiled. Beans and lentils can vary depending on soaking and cooking time. The {% include glossary.html id="carbohydrates" %} did not magically appear or disappear; the water changed the weight.

For dry shelf-stable foods, dry weight is usually the cleanest standard. If you weigh 80 grams of dry rice, pasta, oats, couscous, or lentils, the nutrition is tied to the actual amount of food before water changes it. That is usually more precise than trying to guess whether your cooked portion matches someone else's cooked-weight estimate.

For foods that are already cooked or served cooked, cooked weight is fine. If you are eating leftover rice, restaurant potatoes, canned beans, cooked pasta from a shared pot, or meal-prepped food you did not weigh before cooking, use the cooked weight. Just understand that cooked weights are estimates unless you know how much water was absorbed or lost.

For batch cooking, the best method is simple:

1. Weigh the total raw or dry amount.
2. Cook the full batch.
3. Weigh the final cooked batch.
4. Divide the cooked batch into servings.

For example, if 300 grams of dry rice becomes 900 grams cooked, then one-third of the cooked batch is equivalent to 100 grams dry. You do not need every serving to look identical if you weigh the cooked portions and divide the total accurately.

If that feels like too much friction, use a consistent cooked estimate and move on. The point is not pretending nutrition tracking is laboratory chemistry. The point is reducing avoidable error enough that your {% include glossary.html id="calorie-balance" %} decisions become useful.

The biggest mistake is mixing standards without realizing it: logging a dry-food estimate for a cooked serving, or logging a cooked-food estimate for a dry portion. That can create large errors, especially for rice, pasta, oats, and beans.

A practical default works well: Weigh dry foods dry when you cook them yourself; weigh cooked foods cooked when that is what you actually have in front of you; stay consistent across repeated meals. Precision matters most when the food is calorie-dense, the serving is large, or progress has stalled and you are trying to find the leak.
