The most reliable method is to weigh protein in the state that matches how you planned it: raw or cooked. The important thing is not that one is morally better. The important thing is that raw weight and cooked weight are not interchangeable.

Cooking changes water content. Chicken breast, steak, pork tenderloin, fish, shrimp, tofu, and ground meat can all lose water during cooking, so the cooked portion often weighs less than the raw portion, even though most of the {% include glossary.html id="protein" %} and calories are still there. A 6-ounce raw chicken breast might become roughly 4 to 5 ounces cooked depending on cooking method. That does not mean protein disappeared. It mostly means water left.

If you are cooking for yourself, raw weight is usually the cleanest standard. Weigh the meat, fish, eggs, tofu, or other protein source before cooking, then use that as the planned amount. Raw weight is especially useful when cooking methods vary: grilling, baking, air frying, pan searing, slow cooking, and sous vide can all produce different cooked weights.

If you only have cooked food in front of you, cooked weight is fine. Leftovers, restaurant meals, rotisserie chicken, deli meat, meal-prepped food someone else made, and cooked meat from a shared dish can all be estimated from cooked weight. Just understand that cooked weight is less precise unless you know how much water was lost during cooking.

For batch cooking, use the same basic method:

1. Weigh the total raw protein.
2. Cook the full batch.
3. Weigh the final cooked batch.
4. Divide the cooked batch into servings.

For example, if 1000 grams of raw chicken breast becomes 750 grams cooked, then 250 grams of the cooked batch represents one-third of the original raw batch. You can divide the finished food accurately without pretending that raw and cooked weights are the same thing.

Ground meat needs a little extra attention because fat can render out during cooking. If you drain the fat, some calories leave the food. If you cook the meat into a sauce and keep the drippings, those calories are still in the meal. The same principle applies to fatty cuts of meat: Water loss changes weight, and fat loss depends on whether the rendered fat stays in the dish.

The biggest mistake is mixing standards without noticing it: planning raw chicken but weighing cooked chicken as though it were raw, or treating cooked steak like raw steak. That can create systematic tracking error, especially during a {% include glossary.html id="cut" %}, when small errors can add up.

A practical default works well: Weigh protein raw when you cook it yourself; weigh it cooked when that is what you actually have; use the same method for repeated meals. Precision matters most when the portion is large, the food is fatty, or your progress is not matching your expected {% include glossary.html id="calorie-balance" %}.
